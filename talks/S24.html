Trust: What AI Does and Doesn't Change about Historical Research and Teaching

Louis Hyman (Johns Hopkins University)

Friday, October 16, 2026, 1:30 to 2:30 Eastern, Room 110

Watch on YouTube

Abstract

Louis Hyman argues that artificial intelligence (AI) can restore trust in historical research when language models are used to write inspectable, reproducible code and not to answer questions directly, and he describes the model as an overenthusiastic intern and not an oracle. Drawing on his Johns Hopkins course Introduction to Computational History, he shows how students learn to read Python and to judge when a number is too clean to be true, as with the zero books recorded for California in the 1850 census. In his research he used agentic coding to find a little over 1,100 transactions by the slave trader John McDonogh in New Orleans notarial records, and he describes fine-tuning an open-weight Qwen model with low-rank adaptation (LoRA) on railroad forms. Questions cover digitization and archive funding, and how accuracy is measured against hand-corrected ground truth.
