What Machines Can't Read: Unicode and the Politics of Open-Ended Character Sets

Raja Adal (University of Pittsburgh)

Friday, October 16, 2026, 11:30 to 12:30 Eastern, Room 107

Watch on YouTube

Abstract

Adal traces who decides what machines can read through the history of character encoding, from national standards such as ASCII (American Standard Code for Information Interchange) and four large East Asian standards to Unicode and its 1991 merger with ISO/IEC 10646. He describes the shift from a logic of scarcity to a logic of expansion in the number of sinographs, and uses the Frederica Wang problem and China's attempt to encode 897 rare sinographs in its own Guobiao standard to argue that states have returned as primary actors alongside big tech. A character that Unicode has not encoded cannot be searched or indexed and cannot reliably pass through an optical character recognition (OCR) pipeline or an AI model's tokenizer, so he proposes a digital Zomia of marks, such as marginalia and graffiti, that remain outside the standard. In questions he covers fonts for simplified and traditional characters, emoji, code breaking, and the 172 scripts in Unicode 18.
