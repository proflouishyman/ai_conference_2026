About the Conference

The AI and History Conference 2026 is a practitioner-focused convening for historians at all career stages who are working with, teaching, or trying to evaluate AI and computational methods in their research and institutions. This event is anchored squarely in historical practice: its organizing questions are what AI can do for historians specifically, and what structural, methodological, and professional changes the discipline needs to make computational methods sustainable and rigorous.

The conference addresses the full range of experience, from historians sent by their departments to report back on AI, to scholars already producing advanced computational work, by running sessions leveled for different backgrounds in parallel rather than forcing everyone into the same room. It treats AI as consequential on two fronts at once: as a tool for large-scale research that was previously impossible for a single historian to undertake, and as a way to rejuvenate the classroom. Attendees should leave with a network of peers, a clearer sense of the field's best current work, and concrete skills or project plans they can use immediately.

3:30 – 4:30 13 Network Analysis for Historians: Mapping Social, Political, and Cultural Connections Song Chen (Bucknell University)
Network analysis is a form of data modeling that helps reveal structural patterns in messy and disparate historical evidence — useful for kinship ties and political alliances, but also for subjects like religious cultures and their interactions. Drawing on his work on elite marriage, migration, and temple cults in Tang- and Song-dynasty China, Chen shows how to move from historical sources to network graphs and metrics, what interpretive questions network analysis can and cannot answer, what tools historians can use without advanced programming, and how AI is transforming network data collection, visualization, and analysis.

3:30 – 4:30 14 AI in the Archive: Live Demonstration Loren Moulds (University of Virginia School of Law)
A live walkthrough of the multi-pass AI pipeline Moulds has built for a Mellon-funded project (with UNC) developing tools to help archivists and historians process large collections at scale. His case study: tens of thousands of printed Scottish Court of Session documents (17th-18th century), moved from individual manuscripts to a searchable, richly tagged dataset using a chain of OCR and AI passes — traditional OCR for page geometry and bounding boxes, frontier and open-weight models for text correction, document-boundary detection, and case clustering. Every extracted data point carries full provenance: which model produced it, on what date, with what prompt, and how it links back to the original page. Honest about what's changed methodologically in the last decade, not just what's possible now.

11:30 – 12:30 25 What Machines Can't Read: Unicode and the Politics of Open-Ended Character Sets Raja Adal (University of Pittsburgh)
A character that Unicode has not encoded cannot be searched, indexed, or reliably passed through an OCR pipeline or an AI model's tokenizer. For the Latin alphabet, which has an effectively stable repertoire, this is a limited concern. For open-ended scripts it is not. The encoding of new Sinographs has been a flashpoint since the Unicode Standard's inception in the early 1990s. Emoji, also an open-ended set, acquired a formal annual proposal process. The marks that never did, such as marginalia, graffiti, and scribbles, remain outside the standard entirely. This talk traces who decides, and what those decisions mean for historians whose sources involve world scripts with open and unsettled repertoires or marks that no machine can read.
 OCR Room 107
11:30 – 12:30 28 Environmental History and AI Alexander More (University of Massachusetts Boston; Harvard University)
Climate change is a slow disease, hard to see with the naked eye. Eyewitnesses remember catastrophes and record gradual change only in passing, yet it is the gradual change that sets the baseline for how climate shapes health and economic fate. This session draws on a geo-database of 56,000 records of pre-modern weather and health events, assembled by scholars at every level over ten years and then standardized and de-duplicated into maps of outbreaks, weather, and climate spanning more than a thousand years. The methods run from OCR through GIS to computational frameworks for testing historical reconstructions, and the argument cuts at historians too: the open secret of big data is that we can only see big changes, until machine learning becomes reliable enough to lend a hand without loosening the grip on the primary sources.

 GIS & Spatial Assembly Room
1:30 – 2:30 24 Historical OCR, Named Entity Recognition, and Search Jacob Polay (University of Saskatchewan), Keith Pemberton (Yale University) & Bob Pasker (City University of New York)
Three projects tracing one continuum, from the page image through to the query. Jacob Polay presents a tiered OCR workflow for historians: fast open-source tools across whole collections, commercial services only where needed, drawing on a six-system OCR benchmark run on archival documents from 1612-1921. Most of it runs on a good consumer gaming PC, and agentic coding assistants now let historians set up these pipelines without specialized programming. Keith Pemberton takes up named entity recognition and its historical relationship to OCR, how identifying people, places, and organizations depends on first reading the text accurately, and where the two tasks are converging in modern pipelines. Bob Pasker carries the sequence to retrieval, drawing on Roscoe, a semantic-retrieval system built over roughly eight million digitized U.S. appellate court decisions and used as the infrastructure for his own corpus-scale study of antebellum opinions. His case runs from handwritten ledgers through printed volumes to OCR to embeddings, which makes the archival and evidentiary questions unavoidable: what is lost at each transformation, and what a historian is actually searching when the search is machine-mediated.

Structured Conversations

Assigned seating is used at both structured lunches — Day 1 and Day 2 — with a rotation between them so each attendee sits with a different group each time. Breakfast is casual, open seating, and the dinner on Day 1 is unassigned as well, giving participants a social release valve after a full day of structured programming.

Each table receives a printed conversation guide on card stock. These questions are designed to surface what each attendee is actually working on, what they need, and what they can offer — dissolving the status hierarchies and friend-group clustering that self-seating produces. The most important conversations at this conference are the ones you did not know you needed to have.

Service is family-style: platters placed on tables before guests are seated. Dietary information collected at registration; headcount and restrictions delivered to catering by October 8.

Table Conversation Questions

  1. 1. What brought you to this conference — are you here to learn something specific, or to figure out what questions to ask?
  2. 2. Describe your relationship to computational methods in one sentence, then describe where you want to be in three years.
  3. 3. What is one thing AI has made genuinely easier in your research? What is one thing it has made you more worried about?
  4. 4. What would have to change in your department or institution for computational history to be treated as normal, rigorous historical work?
  5. 5. Who is doing the most interesting computational history right now — and what makes it interesting rather than merely impressive?
  6. 6. If you had to explain to a skeptical senior colleague why this work matters for history as a discipline, what would you say?
  7. 7. What skill or method from this conference do you most want to bring back to your students or your own research?
  8. 8. What is the infrastructure — tools, training, funding, publication venues, collaborative networks — that does not yet exist but should?

Is in-person attendance really full?

Yes. We reached capacity faster than expected. Anyone registering for in person now joins a waitlist, and we will be in touch if a place opens up. Places do open, so it is worth joining.

If I join the waitlist, can I still attend online?

Yes, and we would encourage it. Choose "Online, and add me to the in-person waitlist" on the registration form. That registers you for online access immediately and keeps your place in line, so you are guaranteed to be able to attend either way.

How much does it cost?

Online attendance is free for everyone, whatever your category.

For in-person attendance, graduate students attend free, and there is a $100 fee for faculty and professionals. If that fee would be a barrier, you do not need to pay it and no documentation is required. Select the financial hardship category on the form. Nothing else about your registration changes either way.

Which sessions will be streamed?

Streaming will cover select sessions rather than the full program. Which sessions, and how to access them, will be announced closer to the conference. Everyone registered for online attendance will receive those details by email.

Do I need technical experience to attend?

No. Sessions are run at different levels in parallel rather than putting everyone in the same room, so there is programming for people who have never used these tools and for people who build them. Most registrants so far describe themselves as new to this or just getting started.

When does registration close?

September 15, 2026, for both online and in-person waitlist registration.

I have a dietary restriction or an accessibility need.

There are fields for both on the registration form, and we plan catering and room setup against what people tell us there. If something comes up after you have registered, email lhyman6@jh.edu and we will handle it.

Will the sessions be amplified?

Yes. Sessions will be amplified.

If you need more than amplification, tell us on the registration form or email lhyman6@jh.edu. We would rather hear early, since some arrangements need lead time to book.

Is travel funding available?

We are not able to offer travel funding for attendees. See our hotel suggestions and travel guide for help keeping costs down.

If you have already submitted the form twice, do not worry about it. Tell us which answer is the one you meant and we will set the other aside.
