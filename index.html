About the Conference

The AHA AI Conference 2026 is a practitioner-focused convening for historians at all career stages who are working with, teaching, or trying to evaluate AI and computational methods in their research and institutions. Unlike Digital Humanities conferences, which serve a broad interdisciplinary audience, this event is anchored squarely in historical practice: its organizing questions are what AI can do for historians specifically, and what structural, methodological, and professional changes the discipline needs to make computational methods sustainable and rigorous.

The conference addresses the full range of experience — from historians sent by their departments to report back on AI, to scholars already producing advanced computational work — by running leveled tracks in parallel rather than forcing everyone into the same room. A central aim is to break the isolation of the "lone digital historian": attendees should leave with a network of peers, a clearer sense of the field's best current work, and concrete skills or project plans they can use immediately.

10:00 – 10:30 Plenary: "What AI Actually Does to Historical Evidence"
Speaker: Katie McDonough (Alan Turing Institute)
Historical sources — manuscript, print, image, sound — are not the clean structured data that most AI systems were designed for. This opening plenary names the specific challenges and opportunities that make history a hard case for AI/CS researchers, and frames the conference's central intellectual wager: that historians who understand computational methods are better historians, and that AI researchers who engage seriously with historical evidence produce better tools. The session sets the tone by refusing both uncritical enthusiasm and reflexive skepticism, insisting instead on disciplined evaluation of what works and why. McDonough's MapReader project represents the most methodologically sophisticated current example of building AI tools in direct dialogue with historical source criticism.

Speaker: Christopher Phillips (Carnegie Mellon University)  |  Moderated by Louis Hyman (JHU)
The second plenary zooms out from methods to structure: what graduate training, hiring practices, publication timelines, and institutional arrangements would allow computational history to become a durable part of the discipline rather than a permanent margin? Phillips raised the question of structural change as the defining absence from prior convenings; this session places it at the center.

Structured Conversations

Assigned seating is used at all three structured meals — Breakfast Day 1, Lunch Day 1, and Lunch Day 2 — with a full rotation between sessions so each attendee sits with a different group each time. The dinner on Day 1 is unassigned, giving participants a social release valve after a full day of structured programming.

Each table receives a printed conversation guide on card stock. These questions are designed to surface what each attendee is actually working on, what they need, and what they can offer — dissolving the status hierarchies and friend-group clustering that self-seating produces. The most important conversations at this conference are the ones you did not know you needed to have.

Service is family-style: platters placed on tables before guests are seated. Dietary information collected at registration; headcount and restrictions delivered to catering by October 8.

Table Conversation Questions

  1. 1. What brought you to this conference — are you here to learn something specific, or to figure out what questions to ask?
  2. 2. Describe your relationship to computational methods in one sentence, then describe where you want to be in three years.
  3. 3. What is one thing AI has made genuinely easier in your research? What is one thing it has made you more worried about?
  4. 4. What would have to change in your department or institution for computational history to be treated as normal, rigorous historical work?
  5. 5. Who is doing the most interesting computational history right now — and what makes it interesting rather than merely impressive?
  6. 6. If you had to explain to a skeptical senior colleague why this work matters for history as a discipline, what would you say?
  7. 7. What skill or method from this conference do you most want to bring back to your students or your own research?
  8. 8. What is the infrastructure — tools, training, funding, publication venues, collaborative networks — that does not yet exist but should?
